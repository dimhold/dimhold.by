When my laptop fan gets loud I open the process list and look for the build or a browser tab that went wrong. Usually it is one of them. Over the years the fan became a sort of profiler that I listen to without thinking. I never checked what it misses or how late it is.

Genkin, Shamir and Tromer pulled an RSA key out of the sound of a laptop in 2013. Their signal was the whine of the power electronics. For them the fan was a disturbance and they waited until it switched off. In passing they wrote that fan noise obviously tells about system activity but seems to carry very coarse information. Fansmitter (Guri and colleagues, 2016) goes the other way. There malware sets the fan speed on purpose and a phone up to 8 meters away decodes it. I wanted to know how coarse the fan is for a normal build that does not try to send anything.

## What I measured and what I made up

I have no recording of a laptop fan with labels of what was running. As far as I know, in most laptops the fan logic lives in the closed firmware of the embedded controller. One controller whose logic I can read line by line is thinkfan, the small Linux daemon written for ThinkPads. I took its code at commit 7027a2d from December 30, 2019 with the example config `thinkfan.conf.simple`. It has 7 speed levels with a lower and an upper temperature each. It reads whole degrees once every 5 seconds.

The load is real. I recorded it on a 4 core virtual machine with a step of 0.1 seconds. The first version read `/proc/stat` for the whole machine. My "flat 10%" job came out as 18%, because something else on the machine took up to a third of a core. After that every job ran in its own cgroup and I counted only its CPU time.

Between load and fan there is heat. This part is a model with numbers I picked myself. The chip and the heatsink are 2 boxes with a heat capacity each. Power goes from 3 W at idle to 15 W at full load. At the top fan level the heatsink loses heat 2.4 times better than with the fan off. I tuned it so idle sits at 44°C with the fan off and full load settles at 70°C on the top level. To see how much my guesses matter I also ran a lighter and a heavier heatsink and 25 W at full load instead of 15.

## One compile is invisible

The first job was `gcc -O2 -c sqlite3.c` on the SQLite 3.31.1 amalgamation. Here it takes 21 seconds on 1 core of 4, so the load is 25%. Another compiler or machine would change how long it takes and how many cores it keeps busy. For the fan only these 2 things count. In my model the fan did not move, not in any of the variants. A steady 25% is heard only after 99 to 104 seconds.

## 4 compiles are heard early and for long

4 copies of the same compile in parallel take 22 seconds at 95% load. Here the fan reacts 2.5 to 7 seconds after the start, on the first or the second reading. I expected 5 to 10 seconds, so it was faster than I thought.

Thinkfan reads the temperature only once in 5 seconds. When it sees a jump of more than 2 degrees since the last reading it adds a bias of 1.5 times the jump. This is made to react early, but the size of the jump depends on where the reading falls. In one run the reading came 5 seconds after the start and saw 44 to 51. The bias pushed it to 61 and the fan went straight to level 3. In another the reading came after 1 second, saw only 44 to 47 and the bias was too small. 5 seconds later the step 47 to 51 gave level 1. Of 10 positions of the reading clock, 4 sent the fan to level 3, 5 stopped at level 1 and 1 at level 2.

Here I got stuck on the code for a while. After a jump thinkfan sets a shorter pause of 2 seconds, but at this commit the loop still sleeps the full 5, so the shorter pause is never used.

The end of the job is heard much later. The build ended after 22 seconds and the fan kept running for 83 to 110 seconds after that. In my base model the quieter runs stay on longer. At level 1 the heatsink cools slowly and the fan goes down only when the chip is under 48°C. The long job was 6 rounds of 4 compiles over 128 seconds. The fan reached the top level and came back to zero 120 to 128 seconds after the end. With the heavier heatsink it was 221 to 228 seconds, so this tail is the number I trust least.

The heatsink also changes the level. With the lighter one the 4 compiles drove the fan to level 5 or 7, with the heavier one to level 1 almost always. In one clock position it did not move at all. So the spread between levels 1 and 3 is a property of my base numbers and not something I would expect on every laptop.

## How short a burst can be

To find the shortest event the fan notices I ran bursts of full load from idle. For each length I counted in how many of the 10 clock positions the fan moved at all. A 1 second burst never moved it. 2 seconds moved it once in 10 and 5 seconds 8 times. From 8 seconds on it moved every time.

Then I switched off the bias. Without it nothing up to 10 seconds moved the fan, 15 seconds did it 8 times of 10. 4 parallel compiles were heard only after 12.5 to 17 seconds and always at level 1. In my model the bias gives both the early reaction and the spread of levels.

## Bursts against a flat load with the same average

The last pair was 1 second of full load every 10 seconds against 10 milliseconds of every 100. Both ran 120 seconds and both averaged 9.8%. In the base model both are silent. With 25 W at full load the 1 second bursts lift the fan in 4 positions of 10, but the flat load never does. The 25 W is my guess as well, nobody measured it.

## What I take from it

For me the fan is now a profiler with a memory of 1 to 2 minutes in my base numbers, about a minute with the lighter heatsink and almost 4 with the heavy one. For short jobs its level partly depends on when the reading falls. In my model it does not hear a single threaded compile of 21 seconds. I will still open the process list when the fan gets loud, but the job may have finished some time ago.

## What I did not check

- The firmware of real laptops. Thinkfan is one open controller and I used its simple example config. Other controllers may poll more often or have no bias.
- How the speed turns into sound in a real room. I took from Fansmitter that the speed can be heard and did not record anything.
- I chose my thermal numbers myself and did not measure them on a laptop.
- Each job was recorded once. The model is deterministic, the spread in the numbers comes from the position of the reading clock.
