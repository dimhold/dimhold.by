R-4808N is a restricted area northwest of Las Vegas with Groom Lake inside it. In the FAA order its altitudes are one word, "Unlimited". Its hours are "Continuous". In 3 days of ADS-B positions there is not one inside. If it were as busy as the free air around it, there would be 3,998. My script was written to find exactly such places and it marked 0 of its 48 cells.

I like flight maps and I always read the holes on them by eye. An empty patch in the middle of traffic, so probably something is forbidden there. I never counted how many of these patches are forbidden and how many only have no receiver nearby. Last year people found military bases on the Strava heatmap because somebody was running there. I wanted the opposite, to start from the silence and arrive at the map of restricted areas.

The subject is not new. After MH17 there was an empty patch over eastern Ukraine on flight maps. Schäfer and colleagues wrote in 2014 about what the OpenSky receivers hear. I did not find a work that checks holes against airspace with a control, but my search was short.

## What I took

The positions are from the ADS-B Exchange archive, because it does not hide military and blocked aircraft. I took one snapshot per minute for June 1, July 1 and August 1, 2019. Without aircraft on the ground and positions older than 10 seconds it is 11,629,065 positions. The box around the contiguous US also has a strip of Canada and of Mexico in it.

The areas are from FAA Order JO 7400.10A of February 16, 2019, the yearly list of special use airspace. It is a PDF, so I parsed the text and took the shapes from the FAA open data layer. A shape is accepted if the coordinates printed in the order lie on it, for the 71 shapes with arcs the check is looser. 407 of 454 areas passed, 8 of them prohibited and the others restricted. My first parser lost 8 areas because of a capital letter in "Designated Altitudes", one of them P-51, the submarine base in Bangor. Then 3 more turned up missing, for 2 of them the order itself prints "Designate altitudes" and "Designation altitudes".

The rule was written before the run. The grid is 0.1 degree. A hole is a cell with less than 10% of the median of the ring of cells at distance from 3 to 6, if that median is at least 20 positions. Neighbouring cells are joined. I did it separately below 10,000 ft and above 25,000 ft. At the bottom I expected mountains and missing receivers. At the top I expected few holes and more than a third of them restricted areas, because at that height an aircraft is heard from very far.

## The wrong layer

As a control I moved all the areas together by 1 to 2 degrees in a random direction and counted again, 200 times.

| | below 10,000 ft | above 25,000 ft |
|---|---|---|
| holes | 87 | 57 |
| at least half in a restricted area | 13 | 5 |
| the same for moved areas, mean of 200 | 2.4 | 4.6 |
| the same, maximum of 200 | 8 | 9 |
| outside, filled by lost tracks | 32 | 14 |
| neither | 42 | 38 |

It came out upside down. Above 25,000 ft the real areas do no better than random ones, in 106 of 200 moves there were 5 or more hits. Below 10,000 ft there are 13 hits and no move gave more than 8. Fort Hood and Camp Pendleton are among them. The 3 largest high holes lie near the Mexican border between San Diego and Yuma, one of them on the Mexican side. I don't know why.

"Lost tracks" is my check for reception. If an aircraft disappears for 2 to 20 minutes and comes back in the same layer, I put the missing minutes on the straight line between. If a hole with these minutes is over the 10% line, with the ring counted the same way, I call it a reception hole.

My first count said that 13.2% of minutes are lost at the bottom and 14.3% at the top. That was wrong. The snapshots come about 31 seconds past the minute with some jitter and I numbered them by whole 60 seconds from the first one. From 8 to 12% of the minutes of a day stayed empty and their neighbours had 2 snapshots. After the fix it is 9.5% and 8.6%. I was sure that at the top almost everything is heard, but part of the loss is my own 10 second filter.

P-51 in Bangor has 1 position against 54 expected, P-40 around Camp David 20 against 104. For P-56 over the White House and the Capitol I expected less than 10% and got 14 against 66. All 14 are 2 helicopters, 11 of them one AS350 at 400 or 500 ft.

12 areas are busier inside than around, for example R-3804C at Fort Polk with 302 against 130, many are open when they are not in use.

## What I didn't check

Silence here means that nobody broadcasts a position. ADS-B Out becomes mandatory only on January 1, 2020 and many small and military aircraft still don't have it. Since July 18 the FAA allows government aircraft to switch it off on sensitive missions, so the silence may grow.

I used only P and R areas. Warning areas over the sea are another part of the order and some of the 42 holes may be there. The altitudes in the order are above sea level or above ground, the positions have pressure altitude and I did not convert. Areas overlap, so 219 is not 219 independent observations. All 3 days are the first day of a month and I don't have a map of the receivers.
