`"prefix_not_a_prefix": 200`. The script tried 200 questions and this is its count of failures, so all of them. It took the same prompt with solved examples, cut it into a constant beginning and a question, tokenized the 2 parts separately and checked that the tokens of the beginning are still there when the whole text is tokenized at once. The place of the cut was the most ordinary one, after the blank line that ends the last example.

I build prompts this way all the time. The beginning with instructions and examples is one string, the question is another one and a blank line stands between them. It is obvious that the beginning should be computed once and its KV cache reused, but I had never measured what it saves. I assumed that the risk is in rounding and did not think about the blank line at all.

## The setup

The model is Pythia-160m, 12 layers, float32, on 2 threads of a virtual machine without a GPU. PyTorch is 2.1.0 and transformers is 4.34.0, both released this week. The texts are from the GSM8K test set, several problems with solutions as the beginning and the next problem as the question. A question with its labels is 63 tokens on average, from 26 to 138, so in the timings I used 64 new tokens.

The idea is old and has implementations. llama.cpp has had `--prompt-cache` since May. The vLLM paper (Kwon et al., September) keeps the memory blocks of a shared prefix so that many requests can use them. I only wanted my own numbers on a model small enough for a CPU.

I wrote 6 expectations before the run. 3 of them held, 2 were half right and one was wrong.

## Time and memory

`model(ids)` in transformers computes logits for every position and the output layer here is 50,304 wide. A prompt needs them only for the last position. If I compare the cache with a plain call, part of the win is just the skipped output layer. So I measured the full pass in both ways.

| prefix | full pass, ms | with the cache, ms | ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 64 | 360 | 213 | 1.7 |
| 256 | 1,010 | 292 | 3.5 |
| 512 | 1,858 | 287 | 6.5 |
| 1024 | 5,127 | 352 | 14.6 |
| 1536 | 8,722 | 386 | 22.6 |

These are medians of 5 runs, with logits for the last position only. The plain call at 1536 takes 10,252 ms and against it the ratio would look like 26.6. The short rows are noisy, at 64 tokens the cached pass took from 187 to 497 ms.

I expected a straight line for the full pass and did not get it. From 512 to 1024 there are 1.89 times more tokens to process and the time is 2.76 times longer. I also expected the cached pass not to depend on the prefix. It almost does not, but 64 tokens with no prefix take 221 ms and after 1536 cached ones 386 ms. The new tokens still have to look at all the old ones. And the first request pays for the prefix in full.

Memory is plain arithmetic, 2 × 12 × 768 × 4 = 73,728 bytes per token and the run gave exactly that. A prefix of 1536 tokens is 113 MB. A cache for 8,807 tokens would weigh as much as the model itself, that is a bit more than 4 full contexts of 2048.

## Is it the same answer

I expected tiny differences in logits. On a prefix of 512 tokens I ran 40 questions with the cache and without it. The logits of the last position had the same bits 40 times out of 40. I did not believe it and cut one prompt of 512 tokens in 26 places.

14 cuts gave the same bits and 12 did not. Very short parts on either side differ, 1 to 8 cached tokens or the last 1 to 3 new ones. Cuts at 200, 256 and 300 differ too and I can't explain them by length. Threads are not the reason, 6 prompts in 1 thread gave the same bits as in 2. My guess is that the matrix library picks another path for other shapes. So I think 40 of 40 was luck with the shape. In another script the prefix had 199 tokens and none of 20 questions matched bit for bit. The largest difference was 0.0076 and before the run I wrote 0.0001, though the logits here reach 830 in absolute value.

The greedy continuation of 24 tokens was still the same in 20 of 20. But I would not promise it. I thought that a good part of the 0.0076 could be a shift common to all logits, subtracted the mean and got 0.0094 instead. The gap between the first and the second token moved by up to 0.0020. In the 40 runs with 512 tokens the closest choice was decided by 0.0017.

## The edge

Then the expectation that I got half right. I wrote that a prefix ending with a space would break, because the tokenizer glues a space to the next word. It does, in 200 of 200. But the blank line breaks in 200 of 200 as well. In the whole text 2 newlines before `Question` are 2 tokens. At the end of a string the same 2 newlines become one token. If the cut is after `Question:` nothing breaks.

I took 20 questions, a prefix of 2 solved problems and compared the greedy 24 tokens with those for the whole prompt.

With the cut after the colon 20 of 20 are the same. After the blank line 9 are the same and 11 are not, after the space 8 and 12. Some of the differences come after the answer, when the model starts to invent the next question. Counting only the text before that, the blank line changed 10 and the space 9. One example is "The total cost is $68 per dozen." against "The total cost is $68." The space turned 10% of the students in a dance class into 25%. All of these are wrong, the model is too small for such problems. Still it is a different answer to the same text.

I wanted to be sure about the blame, so I joined the token ids of the 2 pieces and ran them in one pass with no prefix cache. The continuations were the same as with the cache in 60 of 60, it is the tokens. Scott Lundberg wrote in May about prompt boundaries and the guidance library has token healing for them. The cache just makes you cut. Now I tokenize the whole prompt, compare its ids with the cached ones and reuse the longest common part. In my 200 questions this loses one token of 1200.

The same comparison showed another thing. A line with the current time at the top of the prompt, different by 1 second, leaves 13 common tokens of 1276. The same line after the examples leaves 1214. A date at the top, different by 1 day, leaves 7.

## What I did not check

One small model, one tokenizer, float32, 20 questions for every cut and one request at a time. On a GPU with float16 and with batches I would expect more rounding noise, but I have no numbers. I did not measure which of the 2 tokenizations solves more problems. The continuations are 24 tokens, longer ones have more places to diverge. The quadratic part of attention is my guess for the bend after 512, I did not profile it.
