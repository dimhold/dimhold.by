When the GPT-3 paper came out at the end of May, I read table 3.2 first. Its first column is LAMBADA, the task where a model has to guess the last word of a passage. The 175B model gets 76.2 percent there with no examples, the best result before it was 68.0. With 15 examples in the prompt it gets 86.4. My first thought was that this is the point where quantity turns into quality. More than 100 times more parameters than GPT-2 and suddenly a different kind of model.

I thought so before I opened appendix H, where the same task is shown for all 8 sizes. So this post is me checking my own words. First on the paper's table, then on GPT-2, which is the biggest model of this kind I can run myself.

## The plain text line

LAMBADA has 5153 test passages. In the plain format the model just reads the passage and continues it. The answer counts as right if the continuation is exactly the missing word. For GPT-3 this goes from 42.7 percent at 125M parameters to 76.2 at 175B.

If 175B were a break, the last point would jump above the line through the others. It is below. I counted how much accuracy each 10 times more parameters buys. From 125M to 1.3B it is 20.5 points, from 1.3B to 13B it is 8.9, from 13B to 175B only 3.3. A straight line through the 4 smallest models would promise 108.7 percent at 175B, which is not possible. A curve where the error falls by a power law never reaches 100, but from the same 4 points it still promises 86.2. The real number is 76.2.

The paper itself never calls it a break. Figure 1.3 has a caption about its 42 accuracy benchmarks together. It says that zero shot performance "improves steadily with model size" and few shot performance "increases more rapidly". I had seen that caption and still took away only the 175B.

## The line with examples

The second line is the format that gave 86.4. Each example is a passage with the last word replaced by a blank and the answer after an arrow, "a ____. -> step". The real passage comes last, with an empty place after the arrow. The paper explains why: in plain text the model does not know that the passage ends after 1 word, so examples should show it.

With 15 examples the 125M model gets 22.0 percent, 20.7 points worse than with no examples at all. Up to 1.3B the examples mostly hurt, at 760M they give +2.8, at 1.3B again -6.6. From 2.7B on they help every time, by 8.8 to 11.0 points. Somewhere between 1.3B and 2.7B the examples stop hurting for good.

## The same on GPT-2

GPT-3 weights are not public and the API is a private beta, so I can only check the small end of the curve. GPT-2 has 4 sizes, 124M, 355M, 774M and 1.5B, all released by OpenAI, the last one in November. I took 300 random passages from the LAMBADA test set, the version OpenAI used for GPT-2. Every model ran in 4 formats: plain text and the blank format with 0, 3 and 8 examples. More than 8 does not fit, because GPT-2 sees 1024 tokens and 8 examples with the passage are already about 800. The answer counts as right if the most probable token is the right one at every token of the word. There is no stop word filter, so the numbers are lower than in the GPT-2 paper.

The first attempt on the 1.5B model hung. This machine has 8 GB of memory, which does not hold its weights in float32. So I loaded them in bfloat16. After that 3 passages did not finish in 15 minutes. I suppose this processor has no fast path for bfloat16. In the end the weights stay in bfloat16 and every multiplication runs in float32, about 25 seconds per passage with all 4 formats. The other 3 models run fully in float32.

In plain text the 1.5B GPT-2 gets 52.0 percent, the 1.3B GPT-3 gets 63.6. GPT-3 is higher at every size, but the shape is the same. I extended my GPT-2 line to 175B in the same 2 ways as above. The straight line gives 82.3, the power law 72.1 and the real 76.2 is between them.

The blank format without examples gives 0 to 3 percent on every size. It looks like it does not get what the arrow wants. On 14 to 42 percent of passages its first token is a new line. I expected that GPT-2 would lose from the examples, as the small GPT-3 models do. It does, but more than I thought. With 8 examples the 774M model gets 13.7 percent, 35.7 points below plain text. GPT-3 of the same size gets 63.2 with 15 examples.

Then I looked at what the small model actually prints when it is wrong. With 3 examples, in 57.5 percent of its mistakes the first token of its answer is the first token of an example's answer. In 137 of these 158 cases it is the example right above the passage. One passage ends with an officer who "stood at the head of the ____". The answer is "coffin" and the model offers "bliss", the answer of the example before it. So it takes from the examples more than the format. It takes the word itself, mostly from the last one. In plain text the same passages give 0 such matches in 191 mistakes. With 8 examples the copied share falls to 25.9 percent. The 1.5B model copies less, 29.8 percent of its mistakes with 3 examples.

## What I did not check

My GPT-2 numbers are 300 passages per model, one run, greedy. The 95 percent interval around 49 percent is about 6 points in each direction, so some differences between neighbour sizes are noise. The 774M model fits in float32, so I ran it a second time with bfloat16 storage on the first 100 passages. In all 4 formats together, 400 answers, not a single one changed between right and wrong.

I can not say why GPT-3 at 760M reads examples so much better than GPT-2 at 774M. I see 3 differences and did not separate them:

1. All GPT-3 models were trained on 300 billion tokens. GPT-2 was trained on WebText, 40 GB of text. Its paper does not say how many tokens it saw.
2. The paper used 15 examples, I used 8.
3. The paper itself writes that a part of LAMBADA was found in its training data, although its own check found almost no effect on the result.

So my sentence about the break at 175B was wrong in 2 places. In plain text 175B sits on the flatter part of the curve. And the point after which examples always help is about 2 orders of magnitude lower. From my GPT-2 run I can not even tell whether size alone is enough for it.
